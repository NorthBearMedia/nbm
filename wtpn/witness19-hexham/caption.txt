Hexham, Northumberland, 1971 — two boys dug two small carved stone heads out of a back garden. The heads went to Dr Anne Ross, one of the country's leading Celtic scholars, who had spent her career on the Celtic cult of the head and wrote that the head was venerated as the very seat of the soul. A few nights later she woke at two in the morning in a freezing room and saw something going out through her bedroom door. Her own words, on BBC television: about six feet high, slightly stooping, black against the white door, half-animal and half-man — the upper part a wolf, the lower part human, covered with a kind of black, very dark fur. She followed it out onto the landing and watched it go over the banister towards the back of the house. Her daughter came home from school and saw it on the stairs. So did the rest of the household, and the cat. When the heads left the house, she said, it was as if a cloud had lifted. A man in Hexham later said he had made the heads himself in 1956 for his daughter, and a professor at Newcastle found them moulded rather than carved. She never withdrew a word of her account. The heads have since vanished. Dramatised from Dr Anne Ross's BBC interview of 20 February 1976. 🎙️ A Celtic scholar risked her reputation to say this on television.
