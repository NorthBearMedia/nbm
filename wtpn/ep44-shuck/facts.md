# EP44 — Black Shuck at Blythburgh & Bungay, Suffolk, 1577 — verified facts

CONFIRMED:
1. 4 August 1577: a violent storm struck Suffolk during Sunday morning service. At
   St Mary's, Bungay, and Holy Trinity, Blythburgh (about 12 miles apart), people were
   killed inside the churches during the storm. Parish/churchwarden records note deaths
   and storm damage — at Blythburgh the steeple came down through the roof; a man and a
   boy died there. At Bungay two parishioners at prayer were killed.
2. Abraham Fleming, "A Straunge and Terrible Wunder" (printed 1577, London) — the
   contemporary printed account: describes a black dog ("or the divel in such a
   likenesse") running through St Mary's Bungay among the congregation, killing two
   people as they knelt and leaving another "as shrunken as a piece of leather scorched".
   This is the record — REPORT what Fleming reports, attributed ("the pamphlet printed
   that same year says...").
3. The north door of Holy Trinity, Blythburgh, bears black scorch/gouge marks that are
   shown to visitors TODAY as the dog's claw-burns — locally called "the devil's
   fingerprints". CONFIRMED present-tense anchor (T1): the door with the marks is still
   there, still shown.
4. The name: Black Shuck — East Anglia's spectral black dog; the 1577 Bungay/Blythburgh
   attack is its most famous appearance. The Bungay town weathervane depicts the black
   dog. Sightings of a huge black dog have been reported across the Suffolk/Norfolk
   coast and countryside for centuries since ("locals still report" is supported by the
   folklore record; keep wording soft — "people on this coast have never stopped
   reporting him").
5. Folklore attribute: seeing Shuck is an omen — death within the year to the seer or
   someone close. ATTRIBUTE AS FOLKLORE ("they say").
6. May 2014: an archaeological dig at Leiston Abbey (Suffolk coast, ~10 miles from
   Blythburgh) uncovered the skeleton of a very large dog (press: ~7 ft nose to tail,
   est. ~14 stone), buried in a shallow grave near the abbey. Widely reported with the
   Black Shuck connection as the press angle. State the find as fact, the connection as
   the papers' ("the papers only had one name for it").

CUT / DO NOT USE:
- Exact death tolls beyond "two at Bungay; a man and a boy at Blythburgh" (accounts vary).
- "Necks wrung backwards" detail — Fleming's phrasing is contested; use "killed where
  they knelt" instead.
- Any claim the 2014 skeleton was dated to the 1500s (radiocarbon suggested later /
  results inconclusive in reporting) — the find + the press naming is what's usable.
- Claiming the Blythburgh marks are forensically dog claws — they are "shown as" the
  dog's marks.
- Do NOT frame modern sightings as road-ghost encounters (roads banned) — coast, marsh,
  churchyard wording.
